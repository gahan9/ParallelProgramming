       is an incident, not drift.

====================================================================================
WHAT THE FOUR SCENARIOS TEACH
====================================================================================
  1. Only CONCEPT DRIFT necessarily requires retraining. Covariate shift may be
     fixable with reweighting or targeted data collection; prior shift is often
     just a threshold adjustment. Diagnosing before acting saves a training run.
  2. Concept drift is INVISIBLE to input monitoring. Scenario 3 shows stable
     inputs and stable predictions while accuracy falls. If your dashboard only
     watches input distributions, you will never see it.
  3. Scenario 4 looks like severe drift and is a BUG. Stable inputs plus moved
     predictions almost always means the serving path changed, not the world.
     Check deploys and upstream jobs before you schedule a retrain.
  4. Note how the classification changes when labels are unavailable. That is the
     delayed-label problem, and it is the normal operating condition.

WHAT THIS SHOWS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. The greedy loop never showed 240 of 400 items. For those items the model's
     estimate averages 0.500 while their true click-rate averages 0.210, and the
     best one it never showed has a true rate of 0.644. The model is not wrong
     about them -- it is UNINFORMED, and it has no mechanism to become informed.
  2. Observed CTR is not a warning signal. It is measured only on items the model
     chose, so a narrowing model can look like an improving one. This is why CTR
     alone is a dangerous production metric for a recommender.
  3. Coverage is the metric that moves: 40.0% greedy vs 100.0% at the largest
     exploration rate here. It does not appear on any standard service dashboard,
     and it is not a 'model quality' metric in the usual sense. You have to decide
     to measure it.
     Read Gini carefully, though -- it is NOT a coverage proxy. Exploration can
     RAISE it, because sprinkling one impression each across hundreds of tail items
     is itself an unequal distribution. Coverage and long-tail share answer the
     question you actually care about; Gini answers a different one.
  4. In THIS run exploration did not even cost short-term CTR: 0.579 at 10%
     exploration versus 0.576 for the greedy loop. The greedy policy locked onto
     a suboptimal set early and could never discover it had done so, which is
     the whole failure mode. Do not generalise the sign of this result -- with a
     different seed or a stronger prior, exploration usually does cost a little.
     Generalise the mechanism, not the number.

  COUNTERMEASURES this simulation is an argument for:
    * Exploration: epsilon-greedy, Thompson sampling, or an explicit random slate
      slot. Thompson sampling explores far more efficiently than epsilon-greedy;
      epsilon is used here because it is the clearest to reason about.
    * LOG THE PROPENSITY of every impression. Without it you cannot compute an
      unbiased off-policy estimate later, and you will not be able to answer
      'would the other model have done better?' from logs alone.
    * Keep a small randomized holdout population, permanently unaffected by the
      model, as ground truth for what unbiased behaviour looks like.
    * Monitor catalogue coverage and long-tail impression share as first-class
      production metrics, with alerts, not as an annual analysis.

READ THE DIAGONAL. Each row drives ONE column to (near) zero and moves the
others. Enforcing equal opportunity (row 2) zeroes the EO column and generally
worsens demographic parity and precision parity; enforcing demographic parity
(row 3) zeroes the DP column and moves TPR apart. There is no row that zeroes
every column, and that is not a limitation of this search -- it is the
impossibility result.

WHY -- the base rates differ: A = 0.407, B = 0.250
  For a calibrated score with unequal base rates, you cannot simultaneously
  equalize false-positive and false-negative rates AND keep calibration within
  groups, except when the base rates are equal or the classifier is perfect
  (Chouldechova 2017; Kleinberg, Mullainathan & Raghavan 2016).

WHAT TO DO WITH THIS
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
  1. Pick the criterion from the DECISION, not from the dashboard. Ask: who
     bears the cost of each error type here?
       * A false positive that costs the INDIVIDUAL (wrongful denial, wrongful
         flag) argues for equalizing false-positive rates.
       * A false negative that denies an OPPORTUNITY (loan, interview, treatment)
         argues for equal opportunity -- equal TPR.
       * A legally-defined protected decision may impose demographic parity
         regardless of what you would choose. This is where you involve counsel;
         this tool cannot and does not tell you what the law requires.
  2. WRITE DOWN the choice and the reasoning, in the model card, before you
     measure. Choosing the criterion after seeing which one passes is the
     fairness equivalent of peeking.
  3. Report the cost. Row 2 and row 3 both change overall accuracy and change
     who is affected. Present that to the accountable owner as a tradeoff, not
     as a compliance checkbox.
  4. Check whether the disparity is upstream. Different base rates and different
     score noise across groups often come from measurement or representation
     bias in the data, and fixing THAT beats any post-hoc threshold adjustment.
  5. Per-group thresholds are themselves a consequential choice: in some
     jurisdictions and domains, using group membership in the decision rule is
     restricted. Again: counsel, not this tool.
